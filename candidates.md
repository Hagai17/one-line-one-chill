# Candidates — One Line, One Chill (u-azazex-one-line-one-chill-6d4d5488)

Judge criteria, in the kit's published order:
1. A horror story in a single sentence that lands as a scare.
2. Frightens — dread that outlasts the line; the scare is earned, not announced.
3. Vivid — concrete images and specific detail, not vague darkness-and-fear talk.
4. Original — not the obvious mirror, doll, or basement; a turn not met a hundred times.
5. Crafted — tight, rhythm builds, the last word lands the blow.
House note: a line may frighten by what it withholds; gore for its own sake does not count.

## The twelve drafts

1. **Wall knocks** — "For eleven years I have answered the knocking inside my walls with two knocks of my own; tonight, for the first time, it knocked three."
2. **Spare key** — "Someone keeps returning my spare key through the letterbox, polished clean, though I have never lent it to anyone; last night it came back warm."
3. **Drained reservoir** — "The coroner called it a drowning, which was strange, because the reservoir was drained in 1987 and her lungs were full of its water."
4. **Boot prints** — "The searchers found my boot prints all along the riverbank, every one of them walking out of the water, and not one of them walking in."
5. **Dog at 2 a.m.** — "Every night this week my dog has growled at the same corner of my bedroom at two in the morning; tonight he whimpered and hid behind me instead."
6. **Counting breaths** — "I counted my children's breathing through the bedroom wall, the way I do every night: one, two, three — and I have two children."
7. **Baby monitor** — "Every night at 3:17 the baby monitor plays a woman humming, though we have no baby; last night the humming stopped the moment I pressed record."
8. **Behind you** — "You have been reading this sentence for about six seconds, which is exactly how long it has been standing behind you, reading over your shoulder."
9. **Dead narrator** — "The night nurse swears she only tucks in patients who are still breathing; I have been dead since Tuesday, and my blanket is warm."
10. **Lullaby** — "My mother sang the same lullaby through my bedroom wall every night of my childhood; I was thirty before I learned she died the winter I was born."
11. **Ventilator** — "When the power failed, Grandpa's ventilator kept breathing for a full minute after he stopped: in, out, in, out, patient as a tide."
12. **Hiker's photo** — "The last photograph on the missing hiker's camera shows his own tent at night, taken from outside, by someone standing much taller than a man."

## Duel scoring (each pair read in both orders, criteria applied in order)

- **6 vs 10** — Both are known creepypasta frames (counting-twist, dead-parent-twist). 10 wins on craft and a slower build, but both are docked hard on criterion 4. Neither reaches the final.
- **7 vs 5** — The monitor is a stock device and "3:17" is a borrowed cliché; the dog's behaviour change is behavioural and specific. 5 wins: its dread is an escalation the reader completes alone.
- **8 vs 9** — 8 implicates the reader directly, so its dread literally outlasts the line; 9's dead-narrator reveal is a twist the judge has met often. 8 wins on 2 and 4, but is shaky on 1 — it is a trick addressed to the reader more than a story.
- **12 vs 4** — Both are found-evidence images. 12 leans on "much taller than a man," a familiar silhouette; 4's asymmetry (prints only ever leaving the water) is quieter and stranger. 4 wins on 4.
- **3 vs 11** — 11's scare is muddy (machines wind down; why is it frightening?). 3 is a clean impossibility with a date and a body: vivid, earned, withheld. 3 wins clearly.
- **1 vs 2** — The final. 1 has ritual, escalation, and a last word ("three.") that lands. 2 has all of that plus a tactile detail — a key returned *polished*, then *warm* — that puts the intruder inside the reader's own hallway, tonight, with access. On criterion 2, 2's threat is ongoing and domestic; on criterion 5, "warm." is the single hardest last-word blow in the set. 2 wins.
- **2 vs 3** — 3 is the strongest runner-up: forensic, specific, impossible. But its horror is at one remove (a coroner's report about someone else), while 2 happens to the narrator, repeatedly, at their own front door. On criterion 2, 2 wins; on 3 they tie; on 5, "warm." beats "water."
- **2 vs 4** — 4 is a beautiful image but a single static one; 2 builds (returns, polished, never lent, last night, warm) and its final word recontextualises every earlier word. 2 wins.
- **2 vs 5** — 5's corner-of-the-bedroom is close to the stock haunted-room frame; 2's letterbox ritual is fresher, and its withheld intruder is never shown at all. 2 wins on 4 and 2.

## Winner

> Someone keeps returning my spare key through the letterbox, polished clean, though I have never lent it to anyone; last night it came back warm.

Why it wins: a complete story in one sentence (criterion 1); the scare is never announced — it is carried entirely by two adjectives, "polished" and "warm," so the dread is earned and continues after the line ends, because whoever it is has a way in and was just there (2); concrete, seeable detail — a key, a letterbox, a shine (3); no mirror, doll, or basement, and the turn is one the judge has not met a hundred times (4); the rhythm builds clause by clause and the last word lands the blow (5).
